# Medical Care at Sea — Fitness, Care on Board, Medical Chest and MFAG

## At a glance
| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Fitness to work | **ILO/IMO Guidelines on the medical examinations of seafarers**, supporting [[MLC 2006 (ILO)]] and [[STCW 1978]] |
| Care on board | **MLC Regulation 4.1 and Standard A4.1**, with first aid and medical care training under **STCW Table A-VI/4** |
| Medicine chest | Required by MLC Std A4.1 para 4(a). Contents set by each flag State. Reference: WHO **International Medical Guide for Ships (IMGS)**, 3rd edition (2007) |
| Dangerous goods poisoning | **MFAG**, the chemicals supplement to the IMGS |

## Medical examination and certificate
![[diagram:med-validity|How long a seafarer's medical certificate lasts]]

- The Guidelines give maritime administrations an internationally recognized set of criteria for deciding whether a seafarer can safely do routine and emergency duties at sea
- They replaced the **1997 ILO/WHO Guidelines**. A joint ILO–IMO working group developed them, and the ILO Governing Body and the IMO Maritime Safety Committee endorsed them
- Appendices A to E set the fitness criteria for **vision, hearing, physical capability, medication** and **common conditions**
- Validity (MLC Std A1.2 para 7, STCW reg I/9): **2 years** maximum. **1 year** under age 18. **6 years** for colour vision
- A certificate that expires during a voyage stays valid until the next port with a recognized practitioner, for no more than **3 months**
- Examiners issue a shorter certificate only when they can justify it in that case

## Medical care on board (MLC Reg 4.1, Std A4.1)
![[diagram:med-care|Who gives medical care on board]]

- **Ship's doctor:** ships with **100 or more persons** on board, on international voyages usually longer than **3 days**, must carry a qualified medical doctor. Persons are not only seafarers
- **Other ships:** each flag State's law decides which ships need a doctor, considering voyage length, conditions and number of seafarers
- **Ships without a doctor** need at least one seafarer who is in charge of medical care and gives medicine as part of normal duties, or at least one who can give medical first aid. Both must be trained to STCW requirements
- **Medicine chest, equipment and medical guide** are carried on every ship (Std A4.1 para 4(a)). The flag State sets them, considering ship type, number of people and the nature, destination and duration of voyages. The chest is **regularly inspected** by the competent authority
- **Radio or satellite medical advice** must be available to ships at sea through a pre-arranged system
- **Free care:** UK guidance on the Convention says care is at no cost to the seafarer, with a right to go ashore for treatment not available on board where reasonably practicable. Costs can be recovered only in limited cases such as wilful misconduct
- **Records:** completed medical forms with patient details are confidential

## STCW first aid and medical care (Table A-VI/4-1)
The competence is "apply immediate first aid in the event of accident or illness on board". It covers:
- The first-aid kit and body structure and function
- Toxicological hazards on board, including the **MFAG** or its national equivalent
- Examination of a casualty or patient, spinal injuries, burns, scalds, heat and cold effects, fractures, dislocations and muscular injuries
- Medical care of rescued persons
- Radio medical advice, pharmacology and sterilization
- Cardiac arrest, drowning and asphyxia

## MFAG — Medical First Aid Guide for dangerous goods
![[diagram:med-mfag|The three steps of the MFAG]]

- It is the **chemicals supplement to the IMGS**. The Maritime Safety Committee adopted it in May 1998, for use with Amendment 30-00 of the IMDG Code
- Its aim: **initial management of chemical poisoning** and diagnosis within the limits of facilities at sea
- Covers substances in the [[IMDG Code]] and the materials of Appendix B of the BC Code. It covers **accidental** exposure during carriage, not deliberate ingestion. Non-chemical illness goes to the IMGS. National guides apply where practice differs
- **Step 1** emergency action and diagnosis ("Start here"). **Step 2** twenty tables with short instructions for specific situations. **Step 3** appendices: detailed information, a list of medicines, and a list of chemicals by UN number and alphabetically
- First priority is airway, breathing and circulation, then decontamination
- Used together with the IMDG Code, BC Code, EmS (Emergency Procedures for Ships Carrying Dangerous Goods), [[IBC Code]] and [[IGC Code]]

## Medical stores: national examples
There is **no internationally agreed list** of medicines. Each flag State sets scales. Examples:

| Country | Categories |
|---|---|
| **Australia** (AMSA guidance, 2002) | **A:** unlimited voyages, scaled for about 20 crew. **B:** short voyages, generally within 150 miles of a port with adequate medical equipment (200 if within helicopter range). **C:** smooth waters or close to shore |
| **United Kingdom** (MSN 1905, 2021) | **A:** seagoing, unlimited. **B:** under 150 nm (175 nm within helicopter range). **C:** harbour and close-to-shore craft. **D:** seagoing ships with no doctor carrying more than 12 passengers |

- UK: oxygen giving set with a reservoir of at least 400 litres, regulator, flow meter 1–15 litres/min and masks. A checklist is reviewed at least once a year. Ships carrying dangerous substances carry the antidotes named in the MFAG. Doctor required on UK ships with 100+ persons on voyages of 72 hours or more
- Australia: store medicines in date, below 25 °C where possible, never frozen. Some need a locked cupboard, some refrigeration at 2 to 8 °C
