**Salvage 1989 — International Convention on Salvage**

**What is Salvage 1989**
- Rules for salvage operations: rights and duties of salvors, owners and masters, and how rewards are set
- Adds protection of the environment: salvors are paid for preventing pollution even when the ship cannot be saved
- Replaced the 1910 Brussels Salvage Convention ("no cure, no pay")

**History**
- Adopted 28 Apr 1989 (London), after the Amoco Cadiz (1978) showed salvors had no reason to act when little property could be saved
- In force 14 Jul 1996
- SCOPIC clause (1999, industry): a quicker way to pay Art 14 special compensation, used with Lloyd's Open Form

**Applies to**
- Judicial or arbitral proceedings in a Party about salvage operations
- Any vessel or property in danger in navigable or any other waters
- Not: fixed or floating platforms or mobile offshore drilling units on location; warships and State non-commercial ships (unless a State decides otherwise)

---

**Chapter I — General (Arts 1–7)**
- Definitions: salvage operation, vessel, property, damage to the environment (substantial physical damage to human health, marine life or resources in coastal or inland waters), payment
- Art 6: master may sign salvage contracts for the owner; master or owner may sign for the cargo owners
- Art 7: a contract may be annulled if made under undue influence or danger and its terms are inequitable

**Chapter II — Performance of salvage operations (Arts 8–11)**
- Art 8 Duties of the salvor: due care; prevent or minimize damage to the environment; ask others for help when reasonable
- Art 8 Duties of owner and master: co-operate fully; use best endeavours to prevent environmental damage; accept redelivery at a place of safety
- Art 9 Coastal State rights to protect its coastline
- Art 10 **Duty to render assistance** to any person in danger of being lost at sea (master's duty; States must enforce)
- Art 11 States must consider co-operation with salvors (e.g. admission to ports)

**Chapter III — Rights of salvors (Arts 12–19)**
- Art 12: reward only if the operation had a useful result ("no cure, no pay")
- Art 13 **Criteria for the reward** (in no order): salved value · skill and efforts to prevent environmental damage · measure of success · nature and degree of danger · skill and efforts in saving property and life · time, expenses and losses · risk of liability · promptness · availability and use of vessels and equipment · state of readiness and value of the salvor's equipment
  - The reward may not exceed the salved value of vessel and property
- Art 14 **Special compensation**: when the salvor worked on a vessel threatening environmental damage and earned no adequate Art 13 reward → salvor's expenses, increased by up to 30% (tribunal may go up to 100%) if environmental damage was prevented or minimized
- Art 16 Life salvage: no payment from the people saved; life salvors share in the property reward
- Art 18 Misconduct can reduce or remove payment · Art 19 Services after an express and reasonable prohibition by the owner or master are not paid

**Chapter IV — Claims and actions (Arts 20–27)**
- Art 20 Maritime lien · Art 21 Duty to provide security · Art 22 Interim payment
- Art 23 Limitation of actions: 2 years from the end of the salvage operation
- Art 24 Interest · Art 25 State-owned cargoes · Art 26 Humanitarian cargoes · Art 27 Publication of arbitral awards

**Key Point**
- Master may sign a salvage agreement (usually LOF) for owners and cargo — inform the company and P&I at once
- Duty to save life at sea is absolute; property salvage is a separate contract
- Environmental protection is paid (Art 14 / SCOPIC) even if the ship is lost
