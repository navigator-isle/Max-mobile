**Intervention 1969 — International Convention Relating to Intervention on the High Seas in Cases of Oil Pollution Casualties**

**What is Intervention 1969**
- Allows a coastal State to take necessary measures on the high seas to prevent, reduce or remove grave and imminent danger of oil pollution of its coastline after a maritime casualty
- Balances coastal State protection with freedom of the high seas and flag State rights

**History**
- Torrey Canyon grounding (1967): the UK bombed the wreck on the high seas without a clear legal basis
- Adopted 29 Nov 1969 (Brussels) · in force 6 May 1975
- 1973 Protocol (substances other than oil) · in force 30 Mar 1983; list of substances updated by MEPC since
- Later reflected in UNCLOS 1982 Art 221

**Applies to**
- Measures by Parties on the high seas (beyond the territorial sea) after a maritime casualty: collision, stranding or other incident causing material damage or imminent threat
- Not: warships or State ships on non-commercial service

---

**Key articles**
- Art I: right to intervene when there is grave and imminent danger to the coastline or related interests (fishing, tourism, health, wildlife) from pollution or threat of pollution by oil
- Art III **Procedure before acting**
  - Consult the flag State (and other affected States)
  - Notify the persons with interests (owners, salvors) who may be affected
  - May consult independent experts from the IMO list
  - In extreme urgency: act at once, then inform
  - Avoid risk to human life; help persons in distress; do not stop repatriation of crews
- Art V **Proportionality**: measures must be proportionate to the damage, actual or threatened; not go beyond what is reasonably needed; stop when the aim is reached
- Art VI **Compensation**: a State whose measures went beyond what was permitted pays for the extra damage
- Art VIII Disputes: conciliation, then arbitration

**1973 Protocol**
- Extends the same rights to substances other than oil: noxious liquids, other hazardous substances, liquefied gases, radioactive substances listed by IMO, and others that are similarly dangerous

**Key Point**
- After a casualty, a coastal State may order towing, lightering, salvage or even destruction of the ship beyond its territorial sea, if the danger is grave and imminent
- Expect coastal State instructions and co-operate — keep records of orders received
- Linked to places of refuge, salvage (Salvage 1989) and Nairobi WRC 2007
